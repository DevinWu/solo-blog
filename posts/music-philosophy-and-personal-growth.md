## January 5: The Danger of Indiscriminately Receiving All Information

A radio itself is not music. Imagine if a radio received all frequencies simultaneously, with each channel broadcasting a beautiful song—the result would be pure chaos with no aesthetic value. A radio produces music precisely because it's tuned to one frequency, receiving only that single channel's information, thus constantly showcasing one song's magnificence.

Humans are the same. In life, you receive countless messages coming from all directions. Without constraints on what information you accept, you lose focus and become driftwood without direction or accomplishment.

Like a magnifying glass, you must concentrate all sunlight onto one point to generate enough heat to ignite objects. Like fire-starting with a stick, you must balance the wood's rotation to keep friction on one exact point.

## January 6: Focus vs. Non-Focus, Skill vs. Non-Skill

Focus helps you accomplish things well. Yet in certain situations, focus can be counterproductive. For example, when playing music, the melodies that flow unconsciously often sound smoother. When trying to ride a bicycle in a perfectly straight line, not focusing on it makes it easier; the moment you concentrate on staying straight, you naturally veer off.

Skills help redirect an audience's attention and mask deficiencies. Yet for performers, technique must be built upon solid foundational abilities. It cannot replace the fundamentals themselves. A performance can please the eye, but with closed eyes, it must still please the ear.

## January 10: The Power of Musical Intensity

Music influences people. Imagine playing music on stage while the audience chats. You look toward a conversing man, emphasize a few notes, suddenly increase volume, capturing his attention. Then you play a beautiful melody that makes him stand up and listen intently. Gradually lowering the volume, he unconsciously moves closer to the stage. This is one technique for controlling people through music.

There's another technique for earning full applause for a soloist: before reaching the highest note, jump up two octaves, then repeat a single note. While repeating, lower the volume to very quiet but maintain intensity. Hold this single note for 8-16 bars, then in the fourth to second-to-last bar, increase intensity and gradually raise volume back to normal—this will earn applause.

Two details particularly moved me. One: practice isn't something you only begin on stage; you can rehearse beforehand and mentally prepare for potential mistakes and how to avoid them. Two: once you master a technique, it serves you for the next ten years. It becomes truly your technique.

Bass players exist in the stage's background. Through your technique, you enable the soloist to succeed in their performance—that success belongs to everyone on stage. You'll also earn the soloist's gratitude. You need not always be the spotlight. In work, let employees become the stage's shining point while you become the encouragement behind their growth.

Similarly, with other team members, what kind of team do you want to be part of? You can become that kind of person. Become someone who helps others grow! I've clarified this goal. When handling inadequate employee designs, should I criticize or let it slide? Communication and resolution are needed.
